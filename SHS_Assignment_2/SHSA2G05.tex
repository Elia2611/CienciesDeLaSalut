\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

In 1961, Doll and Hill\hyperlink{ref:doll}{[1]} sent out a questionnaire to all men on the British Medical Register asking about their smoking habits. Almost 70\% of such men replied. Death certificates were obtained for medical practitioners and causes of death were assigned on the basis of these certificates. The breslow data set\hyperlink{ref:breslow}{[2]} (attached in the \texttt{breslow.RData} file) contains the person-years of observations and deaths from coronary artery disease accumulated during the first ten years of the study. Such data are shown in Table~\ref{tab:coronary}.

\begin{itemize}

    \item In Section~\hyperref[sec:section4]{4}, you will summarize the results of your analysis.

\end{itemize}

          Among doctors aged 45-54, the coronary death rate was 2.405 deaths per 1000 person-years in smokers (104 deaths over 43248 person-years) and 1.124 deaths per 1000 person-years in nonsmokers (12 deaths over 10673 person-years). The incidence rate ratio (smokers vs.\ nonsmokers) was 2.139, meaning that the coronary mortality rate among smokers was about 2.1 times that of nonsmokers. The Wald test of the null hypothesis of equal incidence rates (IRR $=1$) gave p = 0.0106, so at the 5\% significance level we reject the null hypothesis, there is evidence that the coronary mortality rate among smokers is higher than among nonsmokers in this age group. Since this is an observational study, any association should not be interpreted as causal without considering other potential confounders.
