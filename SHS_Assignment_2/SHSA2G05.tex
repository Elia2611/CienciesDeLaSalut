\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

In 1961, Doll and Hill\hyperlink{ref:doll}{[1]} sent out a questionnaire to all men on the British Medical Register asking about their smoking habits. Almost 70\% of such men replied. Death certificates were obtained for medical practitioners and causes of death were assigned on the basis of these certificates. The breslow data set\hyperlink{ref:breslow}{[2]} (attached in the \texttt{breslow.RData} file) contains the person-years of observations and deaths from coronary artery disease accumulated during the first ten years of the study. Such data are shown in Table~\ref{tab:coronary}.

The aim of this exercise is to analyse the relationship between incidence of coronary deaths (outcome of interest) and smoking status (exposure of interest), taking into account the possible role of age (as a potential confounder or as a potential effect modifier). Specifically:

\begin{itemize}

    \item In Section~\hyperref[sec:section2]{2}, you will estimate incidence rates of coronary mortality and incidence rate ratios associated to smoking status for different age groups. You also will perform a data visualization.

    \item In Section~\hyperref[sec:section3]{3}, you will use the Poisson regression model to estimate the incidence rate of coronary mortality as a function of smoking status, taking into account age as potential confounder or potential effect modifier.

    \item In Section~\hyperref[sec:section4]{4}, you will summarize the results of your analysis.

\end{itemize}

\section{Modeling incidence rates with a Poisson regression model}
\label{sec:section3}



Suppose we are interested in modeling the coronary deaths rate, $Ir=\frac{I}{\Delta t}$ where $I$ is the coronary deaths count and $\Delta t$ is follow-up (in person-years), as a function of smoking status ($E$), which is the exposure of interest, and age group ($A$), as a potential confounder or as a potential effect modifier. 

To do that, we consider here the Generalized Linear Model (GLM) framework. GLM can be thought as an extension of the linear regression model for cases in which the outcome is assumed to follow a probability distribution different to the normal distribution. In the specific case in which the outcome is assumed to follow a Poisson distribution, we have a particular case of GLM named Poisson regression model. Since our outcome, the coronary mortality $I$, is a count variable, it makes sense considering here the Poisson regression model.

Regarding the role of age in the association of interest (i.e., the association between coronary mortality and smoking status), we consider two possible scenarios:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scenario 1:} Age could be a confounder in the association of interest, i.e., when comparing incidence rates between smokers and nonsmoker via incidence rate ratio (IRR), the comparison must be done within the same age group (i.e., we are estimating age-adjusted IRR), and we are assuming that IRR resulting of comparing smokers vs nonsmokers within each age group is the same (i.e., age-adjusted IRR does not depend on age). In this scenario, the Poisson regression model to estimate the coronary deaths rate for individuals with smoking status $i$ and in age group $j$, $Ir_{ij}$ is
  
  
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:modelmain}
\left\{
\begin{array}{l}
\bullet\ \text{Probability distribution:} \\ \\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\qquad
I_{r_{ij}}
=
\frac{I_{ij}}{\Delta t_{ij}},
\qquad
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
I_{ij} \sim  \text{Pois}(\lambda_{ij}),
\qquad
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\Delta t_{ij}\text{ is the follow-up;}\\ \\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\bullet\ \text{Model for the mean:} \\ \\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\qquad
\log
\left(
\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\Delta t_{ij}}
\right)
=
L(E_i, A_j) \text{ (the linear predictor)}\\ \\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\qquad
L(E_i, A_j)
 = 
\alpha + \beta_i E_i + \gamma_j A_j\\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\phantom{
\qquad
L(E_i, A_j)
}
 = 
\alpha + \\
%%%
\phantom{
\qquad
L(E_i, A_j)
} + \beta_1 \I_{i=1} + \\
%%%
\phantom{
\qquad
L(E_i, A_j)
} + 
\gamma_1 \I_{j=1} +
\gamma_2 \I_{j=2} +
\gamma_3 \I_{j=3} +
\gamma_4 \I_{j=4},
\end{array}
\right.
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation*}
\I_{x=a}
=
\left\{
\begin{array}{ll}
1, & \text{if } x = a \\
0, & \text{if } x \neq a
\end{array}
\right..
\end{equation*}

    
    
  \item \textbf{Scenario 2:} Age could be an effect modifier\footnote{We say $Z$ is an effect modifier of the association between $X$ and $Y$ if the effect of $X$ on $Y$ depends on $Z$. For instance, in our data, age ($Z$) would be an effect modifier of the association between smoking status ($X$) and coronary mortality ($Y$) if the age-adjusted coronary mortality incidence rate ratio when comparing smokers vs nonsmokers depends on age.} in the association of interest, i.e., when comparing incidence rates between smokers and nonsmoker via IRR within the same age group (i.e., we are estimating age-adjusted IRR), the resulting IRR is not the same in all age groups (i.e., the age-adjusted IRR depends on age). In this scenario, the Poisson regression model to estimate the coronary deaths rate for individuals with smoking status $i$ and in age group $j$, $Ir_{ij}$, is

  

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:modelinter}
\left\{
\begin{array}{l}
\bullet\ \text{Probability distribution:} \\ \\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\qquad
I_{r_{ij}}
=
\frac{I_{ij}}{\Delta t_{ij}},
\qquad
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
I_{ij} \sim  \text{Pois}(\lambda_{ij}),
\qquad
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\Delta t_{ij}\text{ is the follow-up;}\\ \\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\bullet\ \text{Model for the mean:} \\ \\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\qquad
\log
\left(
\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\Delta t_{ij}}
\right)
=
L(E_i, A_j) \text{ (the linear predictor)}\\ \\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\qquad
L(E_i, A_j)
 = 
\alpha + \beta_i E_i + \gamma_j A_j + \delta_{ij} E_i A_j\\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\phantom{
\qquad
L(E_i, A_j)
}
 = 
\alpha + \\
%%%
\phantom{
\qquad
L(E_i, A_j)
} + \beta_1 \I_{i=1} + \\
%%%
\phantom{
\qquad
L(E_i, A_j)
} + 
\gamma_1 \I_{j=1} +
\gamma_2 \I_{j=2} +
\gamma_3 \I_{j=3} +
\gamma_4 \I_{j=4} +\\
%%%
\phantom{
\qquad
L(E_i, A_j)
} + 
\delta_{11} \I_{i=1}\I_{j=1} +
\delta_{12} \I_{i=1}\I_{j=2} +
\delta_{13} \I_{i=1}\I_{j=3} +
\delta_{14} \I_{i=1}\I_{j=4}.
\end{array}
\right.
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation*}
\I_{x=a}
=
\left\{
\begin{array}{ll}
1, & \text{if } x = a \\
0, & \text{if } x \neq a
\end{array}
\right..
\end{equation*}


\end{itemize}

Note that, in models (\ref{eq:modelmain}) and (\ref{eq:modelinter})\footnote{You can find the \LaTeX\ code to write models (\ref{eq:modelmain}) and (\ref{eq:modelinter}) in files \texttt{mode\_main.tex} and \texttt{model\_inter.tex}, respectively.}, $Ir_{ij}$ is a random variable (different individuals with same smoking status and age can suffer or not coronary death within the same follow-up). Hence, the aim of fitting models (\ref{eq:modelmain}) and (\ref{eq:modelinter}) is to estimate the expectation (i.e., mean) of the coronary deaths rate as:

\begin{equation}
\tag{a}
\label{eq:eqA}
\mathbb{E}(I_{r_{ij}})=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{I_{ij}}{\Delta t_{ij}}\right)=\frac{\mathbb{E}(I_{ij})}{\Delta t_{ij}}=\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\Delta t_{ij}}=\exp(L(E_{i},A_{j})).
\end{equation}


\begin{enumerate}
  \item Note that the logarithm function in models (\ref{eq:modelmain}) and (\ref{eq:modelinter}) implies that we are assuming that the incidence rate varies exponentially with age. Is that assumption reasonable based on information provided by Figure \ref{fig:fig_1}? Why?


No, it would not be reasonable. Taking into consideration that Figure \ref{fig:fig_1} displays the variation of the incidence rate ratio IRR across age groups rather than each individual incidence rate ($Ir_{ij}$), it does not provide enough information to make this assumption. A ratio only informs us about the relative relationship between the incidence rates of smokers and nonsmokers within each age group, not about how each individual incidence rate behaves as a function of age (for which we would need to plot the separate incidence rates on a logarithmic scale).

  \item Prove that, under model (\ref{eq:modelmain}), the age-adjusted expected IRR for smokers vs nonsmokers in group \(j\) does not depend on age and it is:

  \begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eq3}
  IRR_{j}=\exp(\beta_{1}), \quad \forall j.
  \end{equation}

  

Taking the definition of $IRR_j$ based on the expected incidence rates, together with the relation $\mathbb{E}(I_{r_{ij}})=\exp(L(E_{i},A_{j}))$ demonstrated in (\ref{eq:eqA}), we obtain:
  
  \begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
    IRR_{j} &= \frac{ \mathbb{E}(I_{r_{1j}}) }
                    { \mathbb{E}(I_{r_{0j}}) } \\ 
            &= \frac{ \exp(L(E_{1},A_{j})) }
                    { \exp(L(E_{0},A_{j})) } \\ 
            &= \exp(L(E_{1},A_{j}) - L(E_{0},A_{j}))
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}

Now we substitute model (\ref{eq:modelmain}):

  \begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
    IRR_{j} &= \exp(\alpha+\beta_{1}E_{1}+\gamma_{j}A_{j} - (\alpha+\beta_{0}E_{0}+\gamma_{j}A_{j})) \\ 
            &= \exp(\beta_{1}E_{1} - \beta_{0}E_{0}))
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
  
We can observe that the age group term $A_j$ cancels out, implying that the age-adjusted IRR does not depend on age.
Furthermore, since $E_1=1$ for smokers and $E_0=0$ for nonsmokers, we obtain

  \begin{equation*}
    IRR_{j} = \exp(\beta_{1})
  \end{equation*}
  
Thus, proving (\ref{eq:eq3}) and that the age-adjusted expected IRR for smokers vs nonsmokers in group j does not depend on age.



  \item Prove that, under model (\ref{eq:modelinter}), the age-adjusted expected IRR for smokers vs nonsmokers in group \(j\) depends on age and it is:
  
  \begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eq4}
  IRR_{j}=\begin{cases}
  \exp(\beta_{1}), & \text{if } j=0 \\
  \exp(\beta_{1}+\delta_{1j}), & \text{if } j\ne 0
  \end{cases}
  \end{equation}
  
Following the same reasoning as in the previous exercise

\begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
    IRR_{j} &= \frac{ \mathbb{E}(I_{r_{1j}}) }
                    { \mathbb{E}(I_{r_{0j}}) } \\ 
            &= \frac{ \exp(L(E_{1},A_{j})) }
                    { \exp(L(E_{0},A_{j})) } \\ 
            &= \exp(L(E_{1},A_{j}) - L(E_{0},A_{j}))
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}

Now we substitute model (\ref{eq:modelinter}):

  \begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
    IRR_{j} &= \exp(\alpha+\beta_{1}E_{1}+\gamma_{j}A_{j}+\delta_{1j}E_{1}A_{j} - 
               (\alpha+\beta_{0}E_{0}+\gamma_{j}A_{j}+\delta_{0j}E_{0}A_{j})) \\ 
            &= \exp(\beta_{1}E_{1} - \beta_{0}E_{0} + \delta_{1j}E_{1}A_{j} - \delta_{0j}E_{0}A_{j})
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}

Since $E_1=1$ for smokers and $E_0=0$ for nonsmokers, we obtain:

 \begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
    IRR_{j} &= \exp(\beta_{1} + \delta_{1j}A_{j})
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
  
Therefore, the age-adjusted IRR depends on the age group through the term $\delta_{1j}A_{j}$.\\

For the baseline age group ($j=0$) no interaction parameter $\delta_{10}$ is included in the model, therefore 
  
    \begin{equation*}
      IRR_{j} = \exp(\beta_{1})
    \end{equation*}
  
For the remaining age groups ($j\ne 0$) we have 
  
  \begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
    IRR_{j} &= \exp(\beta_{1} + \delta_{1j}A_{j}) \\
            &= \exp(\beta_{1} + \delta_{11}\I_{j=1} + \delta_{12}\I_{j=2} +\delta_{13}\I_{j=3} + \delta_{14}\I_{j=4})\\
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
  
  Using the definition of $\I_{x=a}$ in (\ref{eq:modelinter}), only one  $\I$ variable is equal to 1 for a given age group $j$, while all the others are equal to 0. Therefore,

  \begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
    IRR_{j} &= \exp(\beta_{1} + \delta_{1j}\I_{j=j}) \\
            &= \exp(\beta_{1} + \delta_{1j})
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
  
Which proves (\ref{eq:eq4}). Therefore, the age-adjusted expected IRR for smokers versus nonsmokers depends on age, indicating that age acts as an effect modifier of the association between smoking and coronary mortality.\\


  \item According to Figure \ref{fig:fig_1}, explain what sign (i.e., positive, negative or zero) do you expect for \(\delta_{1j}\) in model (\ref{eq:modelinter}).
 
We expect all interaction parameters $\delta_{1j}$ ($j = 1, 2, 3, 4$) in model~(\ref{eq:modelinter}) to have a negative sign and to become increasingly negative as age increases.

From (\ref{eq:eq4}), we know that $\text{IRR}_0 = \exp(\beta_1)$ for the baseline group ($j=0$) and $\text{IRR}_j = \exp(\beta_1 + \delta_{1j})$ for $j \neq 0$. Since Figure~\ref{fig:fig_1} shows that the incidence rate ratio decreases consistently with age ($\text{IRR}_j < \text{IRR}_0$ for all $j = 1, 2, 3, 4$), we need $\beta_1 + \delta_{1j} < \beta_1$, which implies that $\delta_{1j}$ must be negative and become increasingly negative at every step.
